2 - Overlay Design Pattern Research

January 29th, 2026

Prior to design, modals/overlays weren’t something I ever thought about. Naema Baskanderi mentions users clicking away because they’re used to modals being popups that they just want to get rid of. After reading the article, I’ve learned that modals can be useful and that I’ve already seen plenty of usable modals in my life.

One of the purposes of a modal is to catch the attention of the user while still maintaining the context of the page they are currently on. Instead of going to a new page, the content on the page is “pushed back” or out of focus, allowing the user to focus on whatever the designer/website wants them to see or do.

Like many other design patterns, it's important to keep accessibility in mind for the user. Not only does it allow navigation to more people, it also makes the process less irritating. General rules like making buttons and titles descriptive of course help the user understand what the modal is for, but specific practices can make them better.

The size of the modal shouldn’t take up more than half the page, so the user can still see which page they’re on. This is also why using modals on mobile is not recommended, as mobile screens are often too small. Another good practice to have with modals is giving it a proper focus. The background of the original page being visible is important for context, but could distract from the overlay, which defeats the purpose of it. Darkening it or using a “lightbox effect” is a common way of bringing the focus to the modal while still showing the page.

1 - Research Form Design

January 20th, 2026

Good Form Design requires it to be filled out as quickly and easily as possible for the user. This can be achieved by giving adequate spacing between questions, keeping similar information close together, and providing proper visual contrast. It is also important to encourage the user throughout the process, since forms are typically disliked, it is the designers job to make it less bothersome. Designers can do this by starting with easier questions and avoiding language that may “blame” the user. It is best to assume users won't always read or fully understand questions, so giving clear instructions is important for accurate data.

The article mentions many more suggestions on how to make the process easier for users, one of which is providing a progress bar. It definitely makes the task seem not as daunting since it breaks everything up and I can get a better understanding of how much I have left to do. I also think having a back and forth button is important, as well as clearly specifying if the user is not allowed to go to a previous page. Knowing that I have the ability to go back and change my answers lightens the pressure, which means I'm more likely to finish filling it out.

An online form that I recently had to fill out was BonChon's online ordering. Every section was labeled as required, and when filled out, the label changed to say “completed”. The article mentions this as good form design as it’s better not to let the user assume whether something is required. The label is also set above the choices given, which helps with cognitive load and makes it easier to read/comprehend.
